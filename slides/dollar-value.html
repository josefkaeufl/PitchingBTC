What makes the dollar valuable?

Run the money in your wallet through the same four tests.

Where we already see it
In the US dollar
Scarcefixed or limited supply

Gold, diamonds, your own time — nobody can make more of it on demand.

✗

No fixed cap — supply is set by the Federal Reserve and by bank lending.

Costly to producelabor or energy intensive

Tons of ore moved to refine gold; a diamond cut from rock deep underground.

✗

A $100 bill costs about 11¢ to print; most dollars are digital entries that cost almost nothing.

Irreplaceableno substitute exists

An old photograph, a handwritten letter, a memory — the original or nothing.

✗

Every dollar is identical — and competes with every other currency.

Usefulenables what you couldn't do before

A tool, a car, electricity — it extends what you're capable of.

✓

Legal tender — U.S. taxes are owed in it. For decades nearly all oil was sold in dollars. And borrowers outside the U.S. owe ~$15 trillion in dollars — they must earn dollars to repay.

The dollar's value rests mainly on one trait: usefulness. Demand — from law, trade convention and debt — carries it, not scarcity, cost, or irreplaceability.

Speaker Guide How to present this slide · ~3 min

①Scarce

  • Ask the audience: how many dollars exist — and who decides? There is no fixed number.
  • Supply is shaped by Federal Reserve policy and by bank lending. That flexibility is a deliberate tool — it also means no rule caps the supply.

②Costly to produce

  • A $100 bill costs about 11 cents in printing costs (Federal Reserve, 2025 currency budget).
  • And cash is the small part: most dollars exist only as digital entries — practically free to create.
  • Contrast with gold: tons of ore moved for every ounce.

③Irreplaceable

  • Fungibility is a feature of money: you want any dollar to be as good as any other.
  • The flip side: no single dollar is special, and the dollar competes with every other currency for the same job.

④Useful · land it

  • This is where the dollar is genuinely strong: legal tender, taxes owed in dollars, universal acceptance, reserve currency.
  • Oil: in the 1970s the U.S. and Saudi Arabia agreed that oil would be priced in dollars, and other exporters followed. Importers first had to acquire dollars — demand by convention, not by law.
  • Be precise about today: there are no official figures; estimates put roughly 70–80% of oil trade still in dollars, with the share slowly falling.
  • Debt: governments and companies outside the U.S. owe about $14.7 trillion in dollar debt (BIS, March 2026), roughly 30% of it in emerging markets. To repay, they must earn or buy dollars — often by selling commodities priced in dollars.
  • The compulsion is after the fact: borrowers chose dollars because it is the deepest market, but once they owe dollars, they need dollars. When the dollar strengthens or U.S. rates rise, repaying gets harder — Latin America in the 1980s, Asia in 1997.
  • Concede it openly — the slide does not say the dollar is worthless. It shows what its value rests on.
  • Takeaway: "The dollar's value comes from what everyone agrees to do with it — a network of law and habit."
  • Closing question for any money: "What is its value resting on?"
Print cost: Federal Reserve, 2025 currency budget (variable printing cost, $100 note: 11.3¢). Oil: 1970s U.S.–Saudi understanding; current dollar share is an estimate, not an official figure. Debt: BIS global liquidity indicators, end-March 2026 (USD debt of borrowers outside the U.S.: $14.7 trillion).
